Why Most Sciatica Treatments Don't Last

They chase the pain in your leg — not the compression causing it.

Painkillers & Anti-Inflammatories

These turn down the pain signal, but the nerve is still compressed. The inflammation returns the moment the medication wears off, because nothing structural has changed. Useful for a flare-up — not a solution.

Result: Temporary quiet. The compression — and the pain — remain.

Generic Stretching Routines

"5 stretches for sciatica" is a gamble. If your sciatica is disc-driven, certain stretches push the disc further into the nerve and make it worse. Without knowing the cause, stretching can do real harm.

Result: Hit-or-miss relief. Sometimes a serious setback.

Cortisone Injections

Cortisone reduces inflammation around the nerve and can help for weeks — but inflammation is the body's response to compression. If the compression doesn't change, it comes back, and repeat injections deliver diminishing returns.

Result: Relief that fades. A cycle of repeat injections.

The Real Sources of Sciatic Nerve Pain

Four common causes — each needs a different approach.

Disc Compression

A bulging or herniated disc in your lower back presses on the nerve root where it exits the spine. But the disc didn't fail randomly — years of spinal compression from sitting and weak support created the conditions for it. Decompressing the spine lets the disc retract away from the nerve.

Piriformis Syndrome

The piriformis is a small, deep hip muscle the sciatic nerve runs beneath — and sometimes through. When it tightens from sitting or hip imbalance, it clamps the nerve and creates symptoms identical to a disc herniation. This is frequently misdiagnosed and treated the wrong way.

Spinal Compression & Stenosis

As the spaces in your spine narrow — from prolonged compression, arthritis, or bone spurs — the nerves get squeezed. This tends to worsen with standing and walking and ease with sitting or bending forward. Creating space is the key.

Your Individual Pattern

Disc, piriformis, or general compression — the treatment for each is different, sometimes opposite. The Amari Method assesses your specific cause first, so the decompression work actually targets where your nerve is being pinched.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Help

Most sciatica is painful but not dangerous. Seek urgent care if you have:

  • Sudden severe weakness in your leg or foot — difficulty lifting your foot or walking.
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control — this is a medical emergency (cauda equina syndrome).
  • Numbness in the groin or inner thigh — another sign of serious nerve compression.
  • Progressive neurological symptoms — increasing numbness, weakness, or loss of reflexes.

Frequently Asked Questions — Sciatica

Can sciatica be fixed without surgery or injections?
▼
In most cases, yes. Painkillers and cortisone quiet the inflammation but don't change the compression causing it, so the pain returns. Because most sciatica is driven by compression and muscle imbalance rather than irreparable damage, decompressing the nerve pathway and restoring hip and spinal mobility resolves the pain for the majority of people without surgery.
Why does my sciatica keep coming back?
▼
Recurring sciatica almost always means the root cause was never addressed. The compression, tightness, or weakness irritating the nerve is still there, so as soon as a treatment's effect wears off, the nerve gets pinched again. Lasting relief requires decompressing the nerve, restoring mobility, rebuilding support, and maintaining it with a short daily practice.
Should I rest or move with sciatica?
▼
Movement is almost always better than rest. Prolonged bed rest worsens sciatica by increasing spinal compression, weakening supporting muscles, and tightening hip flexors. The key is the right kind of movement for your cause — gentle decompression and walking help, while heavy lifting and aggressive stretching can make things worse.

How long does it take to get relief?
▼
With treatment targeting the actual cause, most people experience significant relief within 1–4 sessions and full resolution over 4–8 weeks. The timeline depends on how long the compression has been building and how consistently you maintain the daily practice that keeps the nerve pathway clear.
