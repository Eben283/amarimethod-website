The Suspension Squat: alignment by hanging.

Garrett Hewstan February 20, 2026 9 min read
Share f X

There are a few variations of the suspension squat, but they all do the same thing: correct and reset the body from top to bottom.

Most people think of the head and neck as the top of the body. But when it comes to full bodily function, the hands become the functional top of the body while you're hanging, which makes the head, neck, and shoulders the middle of the upper body, not the top.

Simply put, the hands were designed to hang. When you hang, the tension you've accumulated in your neck and shoulders, mostly from sitting, finally gets to redistribute throughout the whole body instead of concentrating in those few areas. The whole body, from the hands down, shares the weight together. No single part doing all the work.

What balance actually is

This goes beyond stretching. Some parts of your hands, elbows, shoulders, neck, spine, and pelvis need to work more, not less. Some parts need to work less, not more. That's what balance is, and it's different for everyone, depending on the body.

In the suspension squat, and in all of Garrett's protocols, you connect to your body's deep wisdom and listen to what your body is telling you, rather than what you've been telling your body to do all along.

Like a suspension bridge

In the suspension squat, the hands are in direct relationship to the neck, shoulders, spine, and the rest of the body. Like a suspension bridge, every part is designed to hold the tension and weight of the whole structure equally. When you're hanging, the hands become partners with the head, neck, shoulders, and spine, instead of those parts carrying the load in isolation.

You can also think of proper function in the body the way you think about a chain-link fence or dominoes. Every part needs to be in direct relationship to the adjacent parts to restore wholeness. When you're hanging, but more importantly, how you're hanging, what's overworking in your body can finally let go, and what's underworking can start to engage with the whole body again.

Almost no one hangs

Consider this: when you lift your hands up in the air and open your armpit, that's the primary movement of the shoulders and hands. When you hang, you're elongating and conditioning the entire body in the frontal plane, as if standing between two walls. Unless you can see your armpit, your shoulder isn't doing its primary movement. Said simply, almost no one hangs. Spinal health and shoulder mobility are directly related to it. It's the shoulder's most basic way to gain functional strength.

Grip strength tracks with longevity.

The reason is proprioception, more parts of your body communicating directly with your brain. The more of that communication happening, the healthier your organ systems tend to be.

Strong lifters can still be weak hangers.

Same body, different conditioning. Some parts have been made to work too hard, which means others have gone quiet. The hands haven't been used in their primary way, and the full chain isn't communicating.

What hanging actually does

Hanging treats the body as one connected system. When that system reconnects, the spine gets real relief: circulation improves at the vertebrae, the discs get a break from constant load, and the muscles that stabilize the spine get conditioned to do their job again. Most people are surprised at what they discover about their own body in the suspension squat.

The way one person needs to hang isn't at all the way another person does.

Common questions.

How often should I practice?

+
Brief, frequent sessions beat long ones. A few short hangs scattered through the day, coming back to the bar between desk hours, do more than one long session. The exact duration and frequency get calibrated to your grip strength and shoulder mobility in your first session, and the practice evolves as your body changes.

What if I can't hang for very long?

+
That's where you start. Even ten or fifteen seconds counts. The grip will give out first because the hands are the part that's been off duty longest. Over weeks, that number grows on its own. Don't push past pain, build over time.

Do I need special equipment?

+
Gymnastic rings hung in a doorway are ideal, since they let the shoulders rotate naturally in a way a fixed bar doesn't. A pull-up bar works too, just less forgiving. Anything you can grip overhead and let your weight pull from will get the chain started.

Will hanging replace other movement?

+
No. Hanging covers an axis most people are missing, but it doesn't replace walking, ground work, or decompression. If you can only do one thing, hanging has the widest impact for the time spent, but it's a piece of a practice, not the whole practice.
