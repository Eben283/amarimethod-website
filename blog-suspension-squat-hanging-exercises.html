Suspension Squat Hanging Exercises: Why Hanging Wakes Up Your Whole Body

9 min read • Last updated: February 20, 2026 • By Dr. Garrett Hewstan, DC

Most people sit eight hours a day, every day, for years. The body slowly forgets what it feels like to be opened. The chest collapses inward. The shoulders round forward. The back goes dormant — not weak, exactly, just disengaged. The whole front-to-back system tilts toward the front and stays there.

Hanging from a pair of rings or a bar reverses that tilt in a way no other movement can. It's the simplest thing in the world — you just grab on and let your weight pull you long. But what happens underneath is unusually thorough.

What's actually happening when you hang

Gravity does the work. When you hang from above, your body is being pulled open along the frontal plane — the same plane that's been folding closed for years. The chest opens. The shoulders externally rotate as the arms reach overhead. The back muscles that have gone quiet from sitting wake up to stabilize you against the load. The spine decompresses passively as the discs get a moment without weight pressing them together.

And the hands are doing something interesting too. They're sending the body a constant stream of proprioceptive information — where am I in space, where is the load, where do I need to brace. That signal travels up the arms, through the shoulders, down the back. The whole posterior chain comes online to support the grip. One movement, every link in the chain participating.

Why hanging works when other exercises don't

Most exercises ask you to force a specific pattern. Bend here, hold there, contract this. Your conscious mind is in charge of the position, and you're working against gravity to maintain it. That's training, but it tends to reinforce the patterns you already have — the dominant side keeps doing the work.

Hanging is different. Once you're up there, gravity is the one organizing your body. Your job is to grip and let go. The micro-movements that happen — small adjustments in the spine, the ribs, the shoulders — aren't choreographed. They're spontaneous, the body finding its own way back toward balance because it finally has permission to.

That permission is the whole point. Most of life is asking your body to hold a position. Hanging asks it to release into one.

Why grip is the entry point

The hands are the first thing to fatigue when you start hanging. That's not a problem — it's the protocol working. The grip is the gateway. Without strong hands, the rest of the chain doesn't get the signal that you're loading it.

Grip strength is also one of the most studied correlates of longevity. Not because the hands matter for living a long time, but because strong hands are downstream of a body that has integrity all the way through it. Weak grip is usually a tell that something further up the chain has been off duty for a while. When you train the grip, you're not just building hands — you're rebuilding the whole communication line from the body to its extremities.

What people experience

The first time most people try this, they hang for ten or fifteen seconds and the grip gives out. That's normal. The hands haven't been asked to do this in years. Over a few weeks, that number doubles, then doubles again. Within a month or two most clients are hanging long enough that the grip stops being the limiting factor.

What people notice first isn't the grip improvement, though. It's the feeling afterward — the upper back lighter, the shoulders sitting lower and farther back without effort, the chest open enough that breathing feels different. The first hang of the day starts to feel like a reset button for posture.

A few months in, clients tell me they feel like they did when they were kids on the monkey bars — playful, capable, not afraid of overhead movement. That's the chain coming all the way back online.

Why this needs to be taught

Hanging looks like the simplest protocol there is. It almost is. But the details that matter most are the ones that aren't obvious from a description: where to position the rings for your arm length, how to engage the shoulders so they don't just pull on the joint capsule, what to do when the grip starts to give out, how to add gentle movement once the basic hang is comfortable.

People who jump in cold often hang in a way that loads the rotator cuff badly — shoulders pulled up around the ears, arms locked, breathing stopped. That's not the protocol working, that's the body bracing. In a session, Dr. Garrett gets the position right, calibrates the time, and shows you what active hanging feels like versus passive dangling. After that, you can do it at home or anywhere there's a bar.

Common questions

How often should I do it?

Brief, frequent sessions beat long ones. A few short hangs scattered through the day — coming back to the bar between desk hours — does more for the chain than one long session. The exact duration scales to your current grip and shoulder mobility, and gets calibrated in your first session.

What if I can't hang for very long?

Perfect — that's where you start. Ten or fifteen seconds counts. The grip will give out first because the hands are the first link waking up. Over weeks, that number grows on its own. Don't push past pain; build over time.

Do I need special equipment?

Gymnastic rings hung in a doorway are ideal — they allow the shoulders to rotate naturally, which a fixed bar doesn't. A pull-up bar works too, just less forgiving. Anything you can grip overhead and let your weight hang from will get the chain started.

Will this replace other movement?

No. Hanging covers an axis most people are missing — the open, overhead, gravity-pulling-you-long axis — but you still need walking, ground work, decompression, the rest of it. If you can only do one thing, hanging has the widest impact for the least time. But it's a piece of a practice, not the whole practice.

What if my hands give out first?

That's the protocol — the hands are the gateway and they fatigue first while they wake up. Between hangs, work on the hand balancer to keep the grip strengthening evenly. After a few weeks the hands stop being the limiter and the rest of the chain starts asking to come back online.
