# Amari Practice: positioning V2

Internal working draft · August 2, 2026

## What Amari is

Amari is a guided practice that teaches people to understand and work with their own body at a high level.

Garrett does not hand every person the same routine. He reads the state a person is in, finds the exact input that lets their body organize differently, and teaches them how to recognize and return to that change. The work keeps changing because the person’s body keeps changing.

Pain may bring someone through the door. It is not the limit of the work. The larger possibility is a body that feels more available, connected, and capable of the life the person wants to live.

## The change someone experiences

People often become used to a body that feels tight, guarded, disconnected, or effortful. Desk work, training, stress, old injuries, and habits create patterns that slowly become normal. They may not remember how different their body can feel.

In a session, a person can feel a rapid state change. A shoulder that has been held for months may suddenly stop hurting. A hip can feel freer. A person can stand, walk, or move with more space. The point is not a momentary sensation. It is direct evidence that the body is capable of a different state.

That experience changes the question from “How do I live with this?” to “What is my body capable of when this pattern is no longer running the show?”

## Why the work is exact

The work is not generic stretching, foam rolling, self-massage, or a familiar mobility drill with better branding. The input has to be exact.

Position, angle, leverage, pressure, breath, timing, and attention work together. A broad version of a movement can do nothing. A small change in angle, followed by the right breath and movement, can create a cascade of release through the body.

That is why people cannot simply watch Garrett once and reproduce the work. They need to learn what the position feels like, how to notice when they are missing it, and how the next version changes as their body changes.

## Why 24 sessions

The Amari Practice is an apprenticeship in the client’s own body. Twenty-four sessions are not a quantity discount or a fixed curriculum.

The first shift can happen quickly. Learning to find it again under the conditions of a real life takes time. Someone can leave a session with a shoulder pain-free, then sit at a desk for a week and start rebuilding the same pattern. The old pattern still has years of repetition behind it.

Over the practice, Garrett teaches the client to recognize the return of that pattern early and to use the exact tools that change it. He also keeps finding the next relevant input. A position that opened something early in the work may be too familiar or no longer appropriate several sessions later.

The useful analogy is martial arts or art. Seeing a technique is not the same as owning it. Repetition with a skilled guide develops timing, discernment, and the ability to make a small correction without guessing.

## The mature outcome: body literacy

The client learns what an available body feels like. They can tell when a familiar desk, training, travel, or stress pattern is beginning to take over. Pain and tension become information instead of a mystery or a crisis.

This is what becoming one’s own healer means in practical terms. It does not mean life will never create pain again. It means a person is less helpless when it does. They know what to look for, they understand the signal, and they have a growing set of precise ways to respond in the moment.

## The practice arc

### Discovery

The person experiences the work and feels that their body can change. They have a direct encounter with a state they may have forgotten was possible.

### Transform

The person learns the exact protocols that apply to them. Garrett refines positions, timing, leverage, and attention while the person develops the ability to recognize their own patterns and recreate meaningful shifts between sessions.

### Awaken

The person can participate more actively in an evolving practice. Garrett continues to see the next layer, but the client has enough awareness and skill to bring the work into training, work, travel, and daily life.

These are directions within a living practice, not a rigid sequence of twelve-step-style lessons.

## Who this is for

The premium Amari Practice is for people with an active, self-directed life who care about what their body lets them do. They may be a triathlete, cyclist, founder, climber, dancer, trainer, parent, or serious recreational athlete. They do not need a professional-athlete identity.

They often already invest in recovery and wellness because they value feeling well before a problem becomes intolerable. They are open to a form of body work outside the conventional medical pathway, without needing to reject doctors, physical therapy, or other care. They are willing to learn from direct experience and take an active role in the work.

For a trainer such as Zach, this can be both personal and professional. His body is part of the instrument he uses to earn a living. Greater body literacy can support his own training and give him a fuller way to understand and talk about movement with clients. That needs to be documented in Zach’s own words before it becomes a public story.

## The high-performance relevance

At a high level, performance is not only strength, conditioning, or perfect anatomy. A great athlete has an unusually refined relationship with the instrument they perform through. They can sense small changes, trust what they feel, and make adjustments that a generic training plan cannot supply.

Amari belongs in that layer. It does not replace sport-specific coaching or promise a podium. It helps someone become less blind to the patterns shaping their body, so their training and effort have more of a chance to express what they are already capable of.

## How the marketing changes

The old front door was ordinary functional relief: help someone get back to a task pain interrupted. Keep that message on condition pages because it is a real entry point.

The premium brand story becomes possibility and mastery. The person may begin with pain, but the story is about gaining access to more of their life, training, work, and performance.

Do not frame this as “for elite athletes only.” A founder who trains for a triathlon does not need to be promised they will win. They need to feel that the thing making full training, recovery, work, or competition feel out of reach might be changeable.

The practical channel mix follows from that: recovery and wellness partners, trainers, endurance and movement communities, athlete networks, and founder/high-output communities. Condition-led SEO remains a separate front door rather than the identity of the premium practice.

## How Garrett and the practice earn lore

Do not call the work mysterious or tell people Garrett is a genius. Show the same credible sequence often enough that people understand there is real depth behind it:

1. A person has accepted a limitation as normal or has tried familiar options.
2. Garrett notices a detail they had not considered.
3. He guides a highly precise input.
4. The person re-tests and feels a clear difference.
5. They learn that this is the start of a longer practice, because there is another layer and the work will adapt with them.

The proof should be specific. A client describing a changed hand position, a before-and-after re-test, or a Marin ride that became possible teaches more than a generic claim about transformation.

The initial proof library should include consented client stories, short re-test videos, later-session stories showing how a protocol evolved, and a few deep profiles of high-output clients. Those stories belong in a founder film, on the premium-practice page, in short social clips, and in partner conversations.

## Private systems thinking

Epstein’s structure-perception-behavior triad is a useful private lens:

- Garrett’s exact input changes the physical state.
- The client feels and recognizes the difference.
- The client learns behavior that helps them return to that state in daily life.

The three Amari sections above and the idea of adapting the input as the client changes give Amari its own usable version. Do not borrow Epstein’s branded terminology or spiritual framework in public material.

## The offer

The $5,400 Amari Practice is not a block of appointments. It is a 24-session guided learning commitment for a person who wants to understand their body well enough to live and perform with more access to it.

The Assessment gives someone a first experience of the work. The Amari Practice gives Garrett and the client enough continuity to develop real body literacy together.

## Claims discipline

Publicly, the work should be carried by genuine, consented client evidence and precise descriptions of what happened. Avoid blanket promises, medical superiority claims, and invented stories. The depth of the method should come through in the work itself.

## ADDENDUM — founder-approved working input from August 6, 2026

**Status:** This addendum captures the material conclusions from Eben’s branding and audience work. It is current working guidance for positioning, identity, and internal staff use. It does **not** turn every statement in the August 2 draft above into settled fact; where this addendum corrects or qualifies earlier language, this addendum controls.

### The method, in Garrett’s actual language

Amari works from a simple observation: **some parts of the body are overworking; some are underworking.** Garrett helps a person find an exact position, angle, leverage, breath, and attentional setup that lets the body feel a different, more available organization.

Feeling good and safe in the position is not a spa-like bonus or a reward after the work. It is part of the prerequisite for the work to unlock. The person needs to be able to feel the movement and the safety of the setup for the body to let go of an overworking pattern and participate differently.

The work can be sophisticated without elaborate apparatus. A doorway, floor, stair step, yoga block, ring, or other simple prop can be enough. The sophistication is in the exact use, attention, and felt setup—not in a machine ecosystem or a specialized treatment room.

### What Amari is—and is not—for the client

Amari is an active guided practice: Garrett supplies discernment and exact guidance; the client moves, feels, learns, and becomes more capable of participating in their own body. It may include an ongoing practice, protocol, membership, or ritual. The distinction is not that Amari has none of those things; it is that the work is not dependent on elaborate equipment or an externally administered fix.

Pain can be an entry point, but the aim is not cosmetic fitness, passive treatment, or endurance-through-damage. The desired state is a body that feels more available, capable, and like home—able to support the life, movement, work, and attention a person wants to bring to it.

### Who this is for

The clearest audience hypothesis is people who use both their bodies and minds at a high level and believe that feeling exceptional in their body is worth investing in. They are often high-achieving or self-directed: they take action, value effectiveness, and are willing to choose something less familiar if it is more useful.

They may ask more of their body than the average person, have the means to invest in feeling and functioning at their best, or deliberately value wellness above other luxuries. They are movers in more than one sense—physically active, mentally engaged, and interested in a fuller life—not necessarily professional athletes.

The relevant blend is action plus attunement: decisive enough to work at something, attentive enough to feel what is happening. This is not a Goggins-style promise to override pain or prove toughness. It is performance through cooperation with the body, not domination of it.

### Brand and identity implications

The visual identity should make the practice feel grounded, exact, human, and capable. It should avoid both wellness-spa decoration and generic high-intensity sports or biohacking cues.

The emerging direction is wordmark-led. No new symbol has been adopted. The legacy interlocking-rings mark reads as wellness/wholeness; the animal and abstract-mark experiments remain exploratory and are not part of the identity. The wordmark needs to carry most of the recognition through one clear, ownable typographic decision: capable but not aggressive, refined but not soft, physical but not medical-tech.

The buyer-world reference set—Tracksmith, Arc’teryx, Oura, Equinox, Aesop, and Remedy Place—should be used as a source of standards, not as a styling recipe. The useful lessons are disciplined identity systems, premium attention, body literacy, exactness, and cultural specificity. Amari should not borrow their mascots, gadgets, wellness-club machinery, palette wholesale, or category language.

For color, begin with an ink-and-paper foundation and add color only when it earns a specific role. Do not return to the prior burgundy/gold identity as the default, and do not use green-and-black energy-drink fitness styling. No final color system has been selected.

### Corrections and claims calibration

- **Re-testing is not an Amari practice.** The earlier references in this V2 draft to a “re-test,” before-and-after re-test videos, or re-testability are stale and must not be used as current Amari positioning or design language.
- “Genuine evidence and precise descriptions” is a useful marketing recommendation, not an already-established brand law. Any public proof still needs to come from actual consented client experience and accurate description of what occurred.
- Eben’s belief that Amari is more effective than physical therapy or chiropractic is founder context, not a public comparative medical-superiority claim. Do not publish that claim without the appropriate substantiation and review.
- Do not invent client stories, protocol demonstrations, or outcome imagery. Generated imagery may support atmosphere or an editorial brand world, but it cannot stand in for documentation of the actual work.
